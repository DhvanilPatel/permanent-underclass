What people are calling the **“permanent underclass” meme** (mostly on X/Twitter, but it’s spilled everywhere) is a cluster of jokes + semi-serious takes built around this idea:

> **AI will make human labor far less valuable, wealth will concentrate in the hands of people who own “capital” (equity, assets, compute), and everyone else becomes a stuck, dependent “underclass.”**
> So you allegedly have **“X years”** to do something—usually *make money, gain leverage, build an audience, buy assets, ship an AI product*—before the window closes.

It reads like a doomsday countdown, but it’s often delivered as **gallows humor / hustle satire**. The “meme” is the *format* and the *vibe*: urgency + class anxiety + AI acceleration.

## The core formats and catchphrases

These are the recurring shapes the meme takes:

* **“You have X years to escape the permanent underclass.”** (The basic template.) ([GitHub][1])
* **“You have 2 years to create a podcast… to escape the permanent underclass.”** (Fame/attention as “escape hatch.”) ([The New Yorker][2])
* **“Ship slop ASAP.”** (“Slop” = AI-generated/AI-augmented output; the message is *lean into AI or fall behind.*) ([The New Yorker][2])
* **Self-parodying backstory jokes** (“dad why are we poor… I sold everything right before the singularity… now we’re the permanent underclass”) ([GitHub][1])

### Mini-glossary that helps decode the meme

* **Compute**: AI “horsepower” (chips + datacenters + inference/training capacity). In the meme worldview, compute is the new factor of production you must “own or access.” ([The New Yorker][2])
* **Slop**: slang for AI-generated/AI-assisted content/products—often low-quality but high-volume. ([The New Yorker][2])
* **“X years” framing**: the key memetic trick. Put a deadline on vague economic anxiety → it becomes emotionally actionable and shareable.

## Where it seems to start (as a meme) vs. where the *idea* comes from

### The meme’s visible early footprint: Aug 2024 Tech/Finance Twitter

A screenshot collage compiled in a later writeup shows several high-engagement tweets using “permanent underclass” in this AI-and-capital sense by **August 2024**:

* **Aug 5, 2024**: “dad why are we poor… sold everything right before the technological singularity… now we’re part of the permanent underclass.” (~5.9k likes / ~402k views shown). ([GitHub][1])
* **Aug 7 (2024)**: “You now have at least 4 years to escape the permanent underclass.” (~7.8k likes / ~781k views shown). ([GitHub][1])
* **Aug 14 (2024)**: A more explicit “theory” tweet: the value of labor collapses, capital runs away, “this is the permanent underclass thing.” (~8.2k likes / ~2M views shown). ([GitHub][1])
* **Aug 15 (2024)**: “we still have few more years to escape permanent underclass” (screenshot). ([GitHub][1])

And later (date shown as **Oct 17**, year not visible in the screenshot), the framing shifts into “career planning” urgency: “you have most of your remaining career to accumulate enough capital to avoid the permanent underclass” (screenshot shows ~2.7k likes / ~99k views). ([GitHub][1])

That’s basically the meme crystallizing: **AI takeoff + time window + capital accumulation**.

### The *idea* underneath: “AI automates labor → capital dominates”

The meme borrows from older “capital vs. labor” arguments, but in modern form:

* If **AI/robots become close substitutes for human labor**, the share of income going to “robot owners” (capital owners) could rise toward 1 in extreme scenarios—i.e., the economic game becomes mostly about ownership, not wages. ([Philip Trammell][3])
* A key question is whether we end up in a world where labor remains a bottleneck (**Baumol-like**) or where machines remove the bottleneck (**Jevons-like**)—and the meme *assumes the Jevons-ish world arrives fast.* ([Philip Trammell][3])

That’s the “theory spine” that makes “underclass” feel plausible to some people online.

## Why the meme exploded in 2025

### 1) Mainstream writeups gave it a name and a story

The New Yorker (Kyle Chayka) turned the scattered joke-format into an identifiable cultural phenomenon: an online joke that reflects sincere fear that AI automation creates a new caste-like inequality, where those with capital buy compute and everyone else gets stuck. ([The New Yorker][2])

It also pinned down a “face” of the meme scene:

* It cites the @creatine_cycle account posting “2 years to create a podcast…” and describes the creator as **Jayden Clark**, a SF entrepreneur whose posts satirize tech’s id. ([The New Yorker][2])

### 2) “AGI by ~2027” countdown narratives poured gasoline on it

A big part of why the meme uses *deadline language* is that some influential AI forecasting content explicitly describes near-term timelines.

Leopold Aschenbrenner’s “Situational Awareness” (June 2024) argues “AGI by 2027” is “strikingly plausible,” including the specific claim that models could do the work of an AI researcher/engineer by then (and he frames it as extrapolating trendlines). ([SITUATIONAL AWARENESS - The Decade Ahead][4])

The meme takes this style of thinking and translates it into:
**“If the world changes that fast, you have almost no time to reposition.”**

### 3) Real-world job anxiety made it feel less like sci-fi

Even among AI leaders, there are explicit worries about displacement and “underclass” formation:

* In his January 2026 essay, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei says he worries displaced people could become an unemployed or very-low-wage “underclass,” and he lays out mechanisms by which AI could disrupt labor markets broadly. ([darioamodei.com][5])
* Axios reported (May 28, 2025) Amodei warned AI could eliminate about half of entry-level white-collar jobs within 1–5 years. ([Axios][6])

Even if you disagree with those timelines, the meme thrives because it can point to credible people saying “this could get ugly.”

## How it spread and mutated

### Phase A: Tech/finance Twitter in 2024

* Early tweets (Aug 2024) mix humor + dread. Some posts look like jokes; others are basically “this is what everyone I know believes.” ([GitHub][1])

### Phase B: Cultural mainstreaming in late 2025

* New Yorker profile (Oct 8, 2025) spreads the framing outside the original in-group and connects it to Marx’s “lumpenproletariat” idea (modernized into “permanent underclass”). ([The New Yorker][2])
* New York Magazine’s Intelligencer later references the same “2 years to create a podcast…” meme line while discussing entry-level career disruption fears. ([New York Magazine][7])

### Phase C: “Explainer posts” and “anti-doomer” rebuttals

Once the meme becomes legible, you get rebuttal essays and meta-commentary:

* **swyx** argues the “permanent” part is a low-agency story; while inequality can widen, the future isn’t locked—people can still learn, build, and adapt, and AI can also increase mobility if used well. ([Swyx][8])
* Saxifrage frames it as a Silicon Valley “theory” about being priced out of AI advantages if you don’t adopt tools aggressively, then argues counterpoints (Baumol effects, comparative advantage, etc.). ([Saxifrage][9])

### Phase D: Commercialization / literalization

At a certain point, memes spawn *products*.

There’s now a literal site, **escapethepermanentunderclass.com**, that presents a countdown (“23 months” at the time it was captured), claims most jobs will be eliminated, and says the “only known escape route” is owning assets/equity before automation + policy responses lock people into dependency; it even links an “official merch shop.” ([Escape The Permanent Underclass][10])

Whether you view that as satire, opportunism, or sincere—it’s a classic sign a meme has escaped its original context.

### Phase E: Satire-of-the-satire

Scott Alexander (Astral Codex Ten) wrote a piece explicitly satirizing the “X years to escape the permanent underclass” framing, arguing it preys on relatively well-off Silicon Valley people anxious about not becoming future oligarchs, and that the fear is often misplaced. ([Scott Alexander][11])

## Why this meme “works” psychologically

A few reasons it spreads so well:

1. **It converts uncertainty into urgency.**
   “AI will change everything someday” is abstract. “You have 2 years” is emotionally concrete.

2. **It flatters and insults at the same time.**
   If you’re in the in-group, you “see the future.” If you’re not acting, you’re headed for the underclass.

3. **It matches Silicon Valley’s default moral language: agency + optimization.**
   Instead of collective politics, it becomes: *grind harder, pivot faster, acquire leverage*. (The New Yorker notes this irony explicitly—people “grind” rather than radicalize.) ([The New Yorker][2])

4. **It’s ambiguous enough to fit many agendas.**

   * AI doomers use it to warn.
   * AI hype people use it to sell.
   * Cynics use it to mock hustle culture.
   * Crypto/finance people use it as “buy assets now.”

## What the meme is *really* arguing about, underneath the jokes

When people say “permanent underclass” in this context, they’re usually bundling several claims:

* **Labor bargaining power collapses** if AI substitutes for large parts of white-collar work. (Amodei explicitly worries about displacement across broad cognitive ability bands and an “underclass” outcome.) ([darioamodei.com][5])
* **Capital gains dominate wages** in a high-automation world; ownership becomes everything. (Trammell’s discussion of robots/capital share getting very high in the “Jevons” scenario is the conceptual backbone.) ([Philip Trammell][3])
* **Time-to-transition might be short** if AI progress is steep and self-reinforcing. (Aschenbrenner’s “trendline” framing contributes to the “deadline” mood.) ([SITUATIONAL AWARENESS - The Decade Ahead][4])
* **Policy/redistribution is uncertain**, so people assume the worst and act individually. (This is a recurring theme in mainstream coverage and responses.) ([The New Yorker][2])

## Important caveat: “meme ≠ settled forecast”

Even the more serious sources that feed the meme (Amodei, Aschenbrenner, etc.) are talking about **risk** and **uncertain timelines**, not a guaranteed outcome. ([darioamodei.com][5])

And some critiques argue the meme is “sticky” precisely because it’s rhetorically powerful, not because it’s proven.
