## The "Permanent Underclass" Meme — A Deep Dive

### Origin & Format

The meme format is simple: **"You have X years/months to [do something] in order to escape the permanent underclass."** It started circulating on X (Twitter) in Silicon Valley circles around mid-2025 and was popularized by accounts like @creatine\_cycle. The New Yorker's Kyle Chayka covered it in October 2025, and it's been a steady discourse object ever since.

The tone is gallows humor — half joke, half genuine anxiety. As NPR reported, the posts usually go something like "we only have five months to escape the permanent underclass — you better work hard because you're going to get stuck." The joke format kept mutating: escape via podcasting, shipping slop, learning to code with agents, buying AI stocks, etc.

The catalyst quote that crystallized the serious version came from Nic Carter (Partner at Castle Island Ventures): "everyone I know believes we have a few years max until the value of labor totally collapses and capital accretes to owners on a runaway loop — basically Marx's worst nightmare/fantasy. This is the permanent underclass thing. And everyone I know subscribes to it."

---

### The Core Thesis

The argument has a clear logical chain:

**1. Capital becomes a substitute for labor.** Throughout history, capital and labor have been imperfect substitutes — a factory owner needs workers, a tech CEO needs engineers. This imperfect substitutability is the source of all worker power. Every labor right we take for granted exists because capital owners *need* workers.

**2. AI breaks this.** If compute can do everything a human worker does, workers lose bargaining power entirely. Marx predicted capital concentration but also predicted revolution as the resolution — because workers had leverage. AI removes that mechanism. You can't go on strike if ChatGPT can break the picket line.

**3. Wealth concentrates permanently.** Trammell and Patel formalized this in "Capital in the 22nd Century" (December 2025), arguing that while Piketty was wrong about the past — historically, capital accumulation has been self-correcting because labor and capital complement each other — he will be right about the future. Once AI makes capital a true substitute for labor, the correction mechanism breaks.

**4. The "privatization of returns" accelerates this.** A lot of AI wealth is being generated in private markets which only large and sophisticated investors have access to. You can't get direct exposure to xAI from your 401k, but the Sultan of Oman can. And for the poorest half of Americans, roughly half their wealth consists of real estate — a form of capital almost uniquely ill-suited to benefiting from automation.

**5. The pricing ladder creates an adoption gap.** Michael Taylor (Saxifrage) laid out the escalating tiers: someone running twenty-two simultaneous Claude Code sessions spending $4,600/month is operating at 2,200x the output of a free user. Sam Altman plans a $2,000/month agent to replace knowledge workers and a $20,000/month PhD-level agent. If you're not building AI fluency now, you won't be able to justify the tools that create real leverage later.

### The Major Counterarguments

**1. Baumol's Cost Disease — "Millionaire Hairdressers"**

When a productivity boom creates tons of well-paying jobs in one sector, wages rise in every other sector too, because everyone competes in the same labor market. If AI makes everything it touches 1000x more efficient, the relative value of human-bottlenecked services goes *up*. There are reports of electricians being paid $500k salaries to fill demand for data center buildouts. Tradespeople, therapists, hairdressers — anyone in a role AI can't fill becomes more expensive and better paid.

**2. The "Weak Links" / Slow Singularity Argument**

Stanford's Jones and Tonetti published a working paper showing the economy operates on a "weak link" principle — production requires a chain of complementary tasks, and the economy is constrained by its slowest components. The math is sobering: automating all software tasks with infinite productivity would only raise GDP by about 2%. Even infinitely automating a third of GDP would only raise it by 50%, because the economy is constrained by the remaining two-thirds of tasks that aren't automated.

They also surfaced a striking historical data point: despite the explosion in computing power, the share of GDP paid as returns to computers has actually *fallen* since the dot-com era — from 4.3% to 3.0% — because the price of compute plummeted faster than quantity grew.

**3. Scott Alexander — "You're Worrying About the Wrong Thing"**

Scott Alexander's response ("You Have Only X Years To Escape Permanent Moon Ownership," January 2026) is probably the most memorable rebuttal. His argument has multiple layers:

First, the meme isn't being spread by poor people — it's preying on neurotic well-off people in Silicon Valley, who fret about being merely bourgeois rather than future-oligarch well-off.

Second, the math doesn't support panic. If we get a crazy AI future and the economy grows 100x, anyone with $25,000 in the stock market now would have $2.5 million. You only need 1% of wealth to be redistributed to stay equally well-off. Currently about 2% of income is redistributed via charity and 25% via government.

Third, you don't need to join an AI company — the main thing an AI employee has is stock, and you can buy stock in Google, or get indirect exposure via Amazon and Microsoft.

And his most striking move was reframing the entire question: if these are the last few years of the human era, they'll be like classical Greece and Rome — a sudden opening of new possibilities where the first people to take them will be remembered for millennia. "What a waste of the privilege of living in Classical Athens to try to become the richest olive merchant."

**4. Swyx — "Agency, Not IQ"**

Swyx argues that AI increases class mobility rather than rigidity, and that the "Great Sorting" will now happen based on agency, not IQ. He points to concrete evidence: it's easier than ever to learn to code, start a startup, train and finetune LLMs, and ramp businesses from zero to a billion in revenue.

He also identifies an emerging sub-underclass between coders who can effectively wield coding agents and those who are skeptical and never get the personal "aha" moments — predicting that jobs will bifurcate between "fabulously rich Tiny Teams" who live above the agentic coding line and the humdrum enterprise jobs below it.

**5. Ben Thompson (Stratechery) — The Paradox of Full Substitution**

Thompson argued that if AI can truly do everything, then everyone can have everything they need, making the question of who owns the robots somewhat moot. His second objection: a world where AI is capable enough to replace all human labor yet still obeys human property law seems implausible.

**6. Zvi Mowshowitz — "You're Asking the Wrong Question"**

Zvi pointed out that in a scenario where AI and robots are multiplying without limit as perfect substitutes for human labor, the distribution of wealth among humans might be the wrong question entirely — the more important question is what happens when the share of profits going to some combination of AI, robots, and capital rises to 100%.

**7. LLM Fundamental Limits**

Taylor points out that no scientific breakthrough has come from ChatGPT despite having access to all published knowledge, and that Claude got stuck in Mt. Moon in Pokémon for over 48 hours — suggesting that the gap between strategy and tactics, the messy middle of extrapolating from incomplete data, might require biological intelligence. Ricardo's comparative advantage also applies: even if AI is more efficient at everything, both humans and AI benefit from specialization based on opportunity cost.

### The Cultural Layer

The New Yorker piece identified a key cultural dynamic: the new desirable employee archetype is a "cracked twenty-two-year-old" who might work "nine-nine-six" (9 AM to 9 PM, six days a week). The only way to escape the permanent AI underclass, ironically, is to lean in and hustle in a bot-like way.

NPR noted that Silicon Valley used to advertise work-life balance and remote work, with people playing ping-pong at the office — but now everyone's back in office, adding Saturdays too. The 9-9-6 schedule came from Chinese big tech companies in the 2010s, until so many employees experienced health problems that the government banned it. San Francisco has apparently decided to adopt it anyway.

Kyle Chayka drew a parallel to Marx's lumpenproletariat — a class especially vulnerable to political manipulation due to its grim conditions. He suggested a similar dynamic may already be playing out: fighting to slow the AI revolution would be an admission that you're not good enough to join it.

---

### The "Escape" Playbook (Per the Believers)

The Saxifrage post distills the actionable advice into three buckets:

1. **Own assets** — if it gets harder to convert labor into capital, the amount of capital you have when the transition happens becomes the thing that matters. Own your home, own shares in AI companies, or start your own.

2. **Actually use the tools** — the biggest mistake is trying AI once, watching it fail, and writing it off. These models are moving so fast that things they couldn't do three months ago, they can do now.

3. **Maximize humanity** — when AI can produce any digital artifact, the things it can't produce become the premium goods. Physical presence, human connection, live experiences. Ari Emanuel launched a $2 billion events company explicitly betting on this thesis.

---

### Where This Lands

The discourse has essentially stratified into four camps:

- **Doomers**: It's real, it's coming, accumulate capital and AI skills or get locked out forever.
- **Slow Singularity**: The transition will be real but much slower than the memes suggest, due to weak-link dynamics and complementary tasks.
- **Optimists (Swyx/Taylor)**: The underclass will exist but won't be permanent — agency and adaptability will determine who escapes.
- **Transcenders (Scott Alexander)**: The whole framing is wrong — if this is really the pivotal moment of human history, obsessing over wealth accumulation is like trying to become the richest olive merchant in Classical Athens. Make something that matters instead.
